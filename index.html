INPUT → OUTPUT · TEXTUAL COMPARISONS

Study connections

Start with a passage or story; follow the shared imagery, then check what the sources actually say.

INPUT · DANIEL 7

Four beasts → Revelation’s sea beast

Output: Daniel’s vision describes four beasts rising from the sea; the fourth has ten horns. Revelation 13 presents a sea beast that combines leopard, bear, and lion features and has seven heads and ten horns. Revelation deliberately reworks Daniel’s beast imagery into a composite figure.

The shared imagery is clear; the passages have different literary settings and interpretations. This comparison does not make them the same vision.

Daniel 7 ↗Revelation 13 ↗
INPUT · UGARITIC BAAL CYCLE (KTU 1.5–1.6)

Mot, Anat, and Baal’s return

Output: In the fragmentary cycle, Mot’s enormous mouth and appetite embody death and the underworld. Baal submits and descends; his absence is followed by mourning and parched land. Anat attacks Mot, cutting, winnowing, burning, grinding, and scattering him like harvested grain. El then dreams that Baal lives, and Shapash is sent to find him.

After a seven-year interval in the surviving narrative, Mot challenges Baal again. Their struggle ends with both collapsing; Shapash warns Mot that El can strip him of his authority. Mot yields, and Baal’s kingship is restored.

The tablets are damaged, and translations differ. The story does not explicitly teach that gods cannot die; “seasonal death and return” is a scholarly interpretation, not a simple statement in the text. The user-supplied anthology scan is identified in the source register, but its copyrighted translation is not reproduced here.

INPUT · 2 KINGS 1:2 → MARK 3:22

Baal-zebub → Beelzebul (aka Beelzebub)

Output: “Baal” began as a West Semitic title meaning “lord/master,” and was used as a divine name or title. In 2 Kings 1, the god of Ekron is called Baal-zebub, conventionally “Baal of flies.” The USCCB’s note calls this a derisive alteration of Baal-zebul, “Prince Baal.” The exact history and sense of zebul are debated.

In Mark 3, Matthew 12, and Luke 11, opponents accuse Jesus of driving out demons by Beelzebul, “the prince of demons.” “Beelzebub” is a familiar later/translation variant. The fun textual twist: a rival god’s exalted-sounding title is recast as “lord of flies,” then appears in the Gospels as the demon boss blamed for an exorcism. Jesus answers with the “divided kingdom” argument and points out the contradiction of calling a liberation demonic while their own exorcists do similar work.

This is a traceable name-and-polemic connection through 2 Kings and Jewish/Christian usage. It does not prove that the Gospel episode retells the Ugaritic Baal Cycle or that every later meaning came from a single deliberate insult; scholarship discusses multiple proposed etymologies.

OUR PROMISE

A library built with care

Every reader deserves access without a price on it. That access must never depend on pretending to have verified what we have not.

01

Preserve the distinctions. Similar titles from different canons and editions are not interchangeable.

02

Name what is missing. A catalogue entry does not mean the complete text is available.

03

Respect the source. We identify the edition and check rights before reproducing or narrating a text.
