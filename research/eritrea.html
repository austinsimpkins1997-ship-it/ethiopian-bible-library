Eritrea: church tradition, scripture witnesses, and language context

This note identifies Eritrean sources relevant to the Ethiopian Bible Library while keeping church institutions, language editions, and particular textual witnesses distinct. It adds research leads only; no Eritrean Bible text has been bundled.

Scope: The Eritrean Orthodox Tewahdo Church is a distinct church in the shared Tewahedo tradition. Its evidence belongs in a separately identified Eritrean tradition layer; it should not be silently merged with the Ethiopian Orthodox Church's list or assumed to have identical editions, wording, or rights.

Eritrea is a Red Sea country in the Horn of Africa. Its scripture landscape is multilingual: a UK government geographic reference describes nine significant ethnolinguistic groups across Semitic, Cushitic, and Nilo-Saharan families, and says the country has no official language. It identifies Tigrinya, Arabic, and English as languages that could be considered “national” in use, while explicitly noting they have no official status. The 1997 Constitution's equality clause must be described cautiously because WIPO Lex says that constitution has not entered into force. See SRC-053 and SRC-051.

Education-policy dates need care: the 2016 UNICEF review describes mother-tongue instruction in the first five primary years (with English instruction from year six); a UNESCO IICBA country brief now summarizes native-language use for up to eight years of primary-level schooling, citing an external policy source. These descriptions differ in date and formulation. Until a current primary Eritrean policy text is inspected, the archive records both as source-specific summaries rather than asserting a single current duration. This matters for planning language access, not for determining scripture canon or translation status. See SRC-048 and SRC-063.

What the current evidence supports

  • Canon-page labels: The Eritrean Orthodox Diocese of the USA and Canada posts a 46-Old-Testament / 35-New-Testament numbered list and describes narrower and broader canons. This is institutional page evidence, not an independently verified Eritrean canon edition. The page itself cites the Ethiopian Church list and Wikipedia, calls the narrower canon “identical to the western bible” despite including additional titles, and ends with “Didascalia | Didache”; exact scope and grouping remain unresolved. A separate comparison reports exact label matches and differences by section position only; it does not align works or merge the lists. Read the page-label comparison · Download all 81 comparison rows (JSON) · Download the source transcription (JSON) · SRC-045 · SRC-029.
  • Tigrinya scripture: A university-hosted research library catalogs a 2000 Asmara Tigrinya Bible Society edition titled The Revised Bible with Deuterocanonical Books, ISBN 978-9966-40-984-3, with Eritrean Orthodox Tewahdo participation. The catalog notes that only Tewahdo and Catholic participants worked on translating its deuterocanonical section. It does not give the edition's complete contents, translation basis, or an open license. See SRC-046.
  • Publication history and credit: Senai W. Andemariam's journal article reports a complete Tigrinya Bible distributed in 1957, a revised conventional 66-book Bible in 1998, and a 2000 improved edition with 16 deuterocanonical books. This conflicts with the Four Corners catalog's wording that the 2000 work is the “first Tigrinya Bible version”; this archive therefore does not repeat that “first” claim. The article documents Eritrean translators and reviewers and is important for keeping local contributors visible in attribution. It is historical research, not scripture text or a license. See SRC-047.
  • Languages: Eritrea's 1997 Constitution text says equality of Eritrean languages is guaranteed (Article 4(3)), but WIPO Lex explicitly states that the Constitution was ratified and has not entered into force. Present the clause as wording in the ratified text, not as operative law. UNICEF's 2016 report describes nine indigenous languages and three scripts in its education-policy account; UK PCGN describes nine significant ethnolinguistic groups and no official language. These are scoped, dated source descriptions, not a current census. They support planning for multilingual coverage without treating Tigrinya as a proxy for the country. See SRC-051, SRC-048, and SRC-053.

Recent Tigrinya digital/product leads: BibleFactory lists a Tigrinya Contemporary Bible 2024 product (BBTI7000) described as a 720-page Old and New Testament edition. Its separate seven-page New Testament sample lists the standard 27 New Testament titles and carries a City Bible Foundation ©2024 “All rights reserved worldwide” colophon; that is not permission to republish it. The full product's contents and any Orthodox canon crosswalk were not inspected. A separate Tigrigna Bible App indexed description claims a text/scan/audio service with 66 books, but direct retrieval returned only an HTML shell, so its edition and book list remain unverified. These leads are separate and neither contributes reader coverage. See SRC-369, SRC-370, and SRC-371.

Text and rights boundary

The online Tigrinya PDF attributed to the Bible Society of Eritrea says it was retyped and reviewed by volunteers and may contain spelling mistakes or missing verses. The associated site says the Bible Society owns the Tigrinya text, permits personal use, and asks users to obtain a license for other uses; it separately claims copyright in its PDF formatting. This is not a redistribution license. The 2000 printed edition has its own unresolved rights record and must not be presumed to share the PDF's terms. Neither witness is bundled. See SRC-049 and SRC-050.

A distinct 1998 Revised Bible in Tigrigna print edition is listed by Bible Society and another distributor as a whole Bible published by the Bible Society of Eritrea; one product record gives ISBN 9783438081964, and the distributor lists a 1,140-page second edition. These catalogue records identify a potential witness, not a verified book list or Orthodox canon crosswalk. No print copy was inspected, no digital text is bundled, and the product pages do not grant digital reproduction rights. See SRC-333 and SRC-334.

A separate 2011 PDF titled The Bible in Tigrigna: Old and New Testaments names a community retyping and review team and warns that spelling errors or missing verses may remain. It is not assumed to be the same witness as the 1998 print Bible or the 1991 Tigrinya Common Language Bible PDF. Although the file says “Available publicly without restriction,” it also states ©2011 and supplies no named open license; the associated text-owner page says personal use is permitted and other uses require a license. The statements are not reconciled, so rights remain unknown and no text is bundled. See SRC-349 and SRC-049.

WIPO Lex hosts Eritrea's 2015 Civil Code, which treats translations as protected works and addresses authorization rights. This is one jurisdiction's law and does not settle the worldwide rights of any edition. Permission and territory-specific review remain necessary for an open global archive. See SRC-052.

How this informs the archive

A 2020 peer-reviewed historical survey documents full Bible translation projects in Tigrinya, Tigre, and Kunama; a Blin New Testament completed in 2018 with Old Testament work then underway at that time; and a printed Saho Gospel of John. The Saho Archive adds distinct leads: a John translation printed in 1964 and a Wycliffe Ethiopia publication containing four Gospel accounts in Eritrean Saho. These are translation-history and bibliographic claims, mainly from Evangelical translation work, not evidence that the versions express the Eritrean Orthodox canon. The survey reports a Tigre whole-Bible date discrepancy (1988 versus 1995/96). For all these leads, the edition, script, translation path, contributors, present project status, and rights need witness-level verification. See SRC-054 and SRC-056.

  • Keep separate tradition identifiers for the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo churches, even when a source reports shared canon structure.
  • Represent Geʽez (gez) and Tigrinya (ti) as different languages even though both use the Ethiopic script (Ethi); never call a Tigrinya witness “Geʽez.”
  • Keep the 2000 multi-book edition and the volunteer digital PDF as separate witnesses. The 2000 edition is registered as an unbundled corpus lead with its translation basis explicitly unknown; the PDF is a distinct, error-prone digital-text lead.
  • Before a Tigrinya reader is admitted, inspect a lawful copy's title page and full table of contents; identify which translation team produced each section; map its actual books to the Eritrean Church list; verify chapter and verse structure against the print witness; and secure reuse permission from the rights holder.
  • Prioritize community review and accurate contributor credit. An English-facing archive should not treat foreign editorial oversight as a substitute for naming Eritrean translators and reviewers.

Useful Eritrea-aligned source paths

The strongest next steps are bibliographic and rights work, not bulk importing an unverified text:

  • Locate and inspect a lawful physical or digitized copy of the 2000 Bible Society of Eritrea edition. Record its title page, contents, translators, languages used in translation, and the exact deuterocanonical book list before creating book mappings. The Four Corners record is the catalog lead; Andemariam's article supplies historical context, not an edition collation.
  • Seek permission directly from the Bible Society of Eritrea or current rights holder before reproducing or translating that edition. Eritrea's 2015 Civil Code treats translations as protected works and reserves translation authorization to the author, subject to statutory exceptions. This is a legal research signal, not a global rights opinion. See SRC-052.
  • For wider language coverage, identify community, church, university, and library partners for Tigrinya and other Eritrean languages, then register each verified witness separately with script, edition, contributors, text basis, rights, and review status. The existing sources do not establish a complete public-domain Eritrean corpus.
