# Related ancient works and manuscript leads

These entries are included because they arose in the surrounding research. They are **not automatically part of the Ethiopian biblical canon** and must not be inserted into the 89-title catalogue without a documented tradition-specific reason.

## Ugaritic Baal Cycle

- The **Baal Cycle** is an ancient Ugaritic mythological poem preserved in fragmentary form across six tablet units conventionally catalogued as KTU 1.1–1.6. It was found at Ras Shamra (ancient Ugarit). The Louvre collection record for AO 16643 / RS 3.361 identifies that individual incomplete tablet as a Baal Cycle myth; this does not mean all witnesses are held by the Louvre.
- It is included as **comparative ancient literature**, not as scripture in the Ethiopian Orthodox canon and not as a Dead Sea Scroll.
- The page image supplied in this conversation matches Harold L. Ginsberg’s translation in James B. Pritchard (ed.), *Ancient Near Eastern Texts Relating to the Old Testament*, 3rd ed. with supplement (Princeton University Press, 1969), “Ugaritic Myths, Epics, and Legends,” p. 137. It is a copyrighted anthology translation. The site records the bibliographic identification but does not reproduce the scan or text; reuse rights remain unresolved.
- Tablet order, damaged passages, restorations, and translation choices require critical-edition references. A university-hosted translation resource is recorded as a reading lead, but reuse rights have not been cleared.
- Sources: [Louvre artifact record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010141446), [French Ministry of Culture overview](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/ougarit/fr/le-cycle-de-baal), [Brill scholarly chapter excerpt](https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/9789004294103/B9789004294103-s007.xml), [Marquette University Ugaritic texts PDF](https://www.marquette.edu/maqom/baalyamm.pdf).
- The University of Göttingen's [Edition of the Ugaritic Poetic Corpus](https://eupt.uni-goettingen.de/) is a current digital philological-edition project (transliteration, vocalization, translation, analysis, and commentary). Its work plan places KTU 1.1–1.6 in a later phase; the portal releases editions gradually and remains Alpha. It is a strong future source, not yet a complete online edition of the Baal Cycle (SRC-185).

### Baal Cycle books and translation status

- *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry* (ed. Simon B. Parker) presents the Baal, Kirta, and Aqhat poems with Ugaritic text, English translations, and notes. *Stories from Ancient Canaan* (Michael D. Coogan) is a shorter English-language collection. These are modern copyrighted books; this archive links their catalog records but does not reproduce or translate the books themselves.
- Mark S. Smith's *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*, vol. I, covers KTU 1.1–1.2; vol. II, by Smith and Wayne T. Pitard, covers KTU/CAT 1.3–1.4. These two volumes are substantial critical editions, not a single exhaustive translation of every surviving witness in KTU 1.1–1.6. *The Early History of God* is a secondary historical study, and *Mighty Baal* is an edited collection of essays; neither is a translation of the cycle.
- The intended archive work is a separately authored English rendering of ancient Ugaritic text, not a translation of these modern books. That work must cite the base edition, distinguish visible source text from restored/lost text, retain alternative readings and uncertainty, and receive Ugaritic specialist review before it is labeled verified. No such translation is bundled yet.
- Book records and scope notes are on the [Baal Cycle reading and translation page](site/research/baal-cycle-reading.html). Bibliographic sources SRC-179–SRC-184 are linked to REL-001 in `site/archive.json`.

## Dead Sea Scrolls and Ethiopic books

- **Jubilees**: Hebrew fragments of Jubilees were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. This is evidence for the antiquity and circulation of Jubilees, not a complete English text from Qumran. The complete work is preserved in Ge'ez; the public-domain English translation by R. H. Charles (1902) is a candidate text source requiring edition and rights metadata review.
- **1 Enoch**: Aramaic fragments of Enoch are among the Dead Sea Scrolls; the complete text is preserved in Ge'ez. R. H. Charles's 1912 English translation is public domain in the US but is an old translation and not a substitute for a modern critical edition.
- **11Q11 / 11QApocryphal Psalms**: a fragmentary Hebrew scroll from Qumran Cave 11. It preserves exorcistic/apotropaic psalm material and Psalm 91; Solomon's name appears in a damaged passage. This is not a standalone “Secret Book of Solomon.” Translations reconstruct lacunae and must identify editorial restorations.

## Pistis Sophia

- **Pistis Sophia / Askew Codex**, British Library **Add MS 5114**: a Coptic manuscript, not a Dead Sea Scroll and not a book in the Ethiopian Orthodox biblical canon. The British Library catalogue gives the shelfmark and codex metadata; its images are currently marked unavailable in the catalogue search result. G. R. S. Mead's older English translation is discoverable online, but the source edition and reproduction rights should be reviewed before reuse.

## Solomon-associated works that should not be conflated

- **Testament of Solomon**: a pseudepigraphical work ascribed to Solomon, describing demons and Temple construction. It is separate from 11Q11 and is not itself a Dead Sea Scroll. Dating and composition history are debated; don't label it canonical scripture without a tradition-specific source.
- **Psalms of Solomon**: a separate collection of 18 Jewish psalms, not authored by biblical Solomon according to scholarly convention and not the same work as 11Q11.
- **Key of Solomon / Solomonic magical literature**: medieval magical-text traditions, not ancient Qumran scrolls and not part of the Tewahedo biblical canon.

## Passage connections

- **Daniel 7 → Revelation 13**: Revelation's sea beast reworks features associated with Daniel's four beasts, but the texts remain distinct visions with different literary settings. See the [USCCB Daniel 7 page](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/7) and [Revelation 13 page](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/13).
- **Baal Cycle, KTU 1.5–1.6**: Mot's immense mouth and hunger frame death as an underworld power. Baal submits and descends; Anat attacks and dismembers Mot; El dreams that Baal lives, and Shapash searches for him. In the seventh year Mot challenges Baal again. Their struggle ends with both falling; Shapash warns Mot that El can remove his authority, and Mot yields. The tablets are damaged. The statement that gods “cannot truly die” and the seasonal-death reading are interpretations, not wording that the site presents as settled fact. See the [Louvre tablet record](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010141446), the [French Ministry of Culture overview](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/ougarit/fr/le-cycle-de-baal), and a [secondary scholarly discussion](https://files01.core.ac.uk/download/217242508.pdf).

## Baal-zebub, Beelzebul, and Beelzebub

- **Baal** is a West Semitic title meaning “lord/master,” used for deities and also as a divine name. The Baal of Ugaritic literature is not automatically identical to every local deity called Baal.
- In **2 Kings 1:2–16**, Ahaziah of Israel sends to inquire of Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron, whether he will recover from injury. The USCCB note calls “Baal of flies” a derisive alteration of **Baal-zebul**, glossed there as “Prince Baal.” Other explanations of the original name and the direction of the wordplay remain debated.
- **Mark 3:22; Matthew 12:24–29; Luke 11:14–23** use Beelzebul for the “prince of demons” in an accusation that Jesus casts out demons through demonic power. “Beelzebub” is a familiar alternate form in translations and later Christian tradition. The Gospels do not say that this passage retells the Ugaritic Baal Cycle.
- The textual irony: a name associated with a rival deity is recast as “lord of flies,” then used as the demon chief in an accusation against an exorcist. Jesus answers that a kingdom divided against itself cannot stand and challenges the accusers’ double standard concerning their own exorcists.
- Sources: [2 Kings 1 and its name note](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/2kings/1), [Mark 3](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/3), [Matthew 12](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/12), [Luke 11](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/11), and the scholarly discussion [“The Devil in the Details”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/14/8/1070).
